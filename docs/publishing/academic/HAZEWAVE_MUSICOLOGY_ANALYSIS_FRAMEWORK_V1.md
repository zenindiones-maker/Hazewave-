# HAZEWAVE Musicology Analysis Framework v1

## 1. Purpose

This framework ensures that Hazewave’s history of sound is genuinely musical, not merely social history with music as decoration.

Whenever sufficient evidence exists, the book must slow down and analyze how the sound system actually works.

## 2. Analytical principle

Never write:

> “They had drums and rhythmic music.”

when the evidence can support:

- which instruments;
- local names;
- materials;
- construction;
- stroke technique;
- timbral contrast;
- ensemble function;
- temporal organization;
- social function;
- transmission;
- historical variation.

## 3. Specificity over vague categories

Avoid generic analytical categories such as:
- tribal percussion;
- African drums;
- Indigenous music;
- Oriental scales;
- primitive rhythm.

Resolve toward:
- named people/community;
- local terminology;
- specific region;
- date/period;
- repertoire/practice;
- source.

Broad reader-facing wording can be introduced, then immediately refined.

## 5. Instrument analysis

For each instrument:
- local name;
- translation only when useful;
- organological type;
- materials;
- dimensions;
- construction;
- sound-producing element;
- excitation method;
- resonator;
- tuning method;
- playing position;
- stroke/fingering/air technique;
- timbral vocabulary;
- ensemble role;
- solo role;
- maker tradition;
- lifecycle/maintenance;
- symbolic/social role where evidenced;
- archaeological/history record.

## 6. Organology

Hornbostel–Sachs may be used as a comparative reference.

Core categories include:
- idiophones;
- membranophones;
- chordophones;
- aerophones;
- electrophones in later extensions/context.

But Hazewave must also document local classification systems when available.

The external taxonomy is not treated as the culture’s own ontology.

## 7. Rhythm analysis

Where evidence permits:
- pulse;
- beat;
- subdivision;
- meter;
- additive structures;
- cycle length;
- timeline/bell patterns;
- ostinato;
- interlocking;
- cross-rhythm;
- polymeter;
- syncopation;
- phrase displacement;
- swing/microtiming;
- tempo;
- entrainment;
- leader/follower relationships;
- improvisational constraints.

When scholars disagree on the best analytical model, show the disagreement.

## 8. Percussion depth

For percussion-centered traditions, document:

- membrane/material;
- tuning;
- stroke positions;
- hand/stick technique;
- open/muted/slap-type distinctions only where documented;
- timbre assignment;
- ensemble hierarchy;
- cueing;
- cycles;
- interdependence;
- dance relation;
- speech/drum-language relation where documented;
- learning methods;
- improvisation rules;
- context and repertoire.

Never infer “ancient continuity” merely because a living practice uses an old material.

## 9. Pitch / tuning

Possible analysis:
- pitch inventory;
- scale;
- mode;
- tuning system;
- intonation;
- drone;
- melodic contour;
- register;
- octave equivalence or lack thereof;
- flexible pitch;
- timbre/pitch interactions;
- tuning by instrument/ensemble.

Do not force every system into twelve-tone equal temperament.

## 10. Voice

Analyze:
- vocal production;
- register;
- timbre;
- ornament;
- breath;
- text setting;
- speech-song continuum;
- call-and-response;
- ensemble texture;
- gender/age roles only where supported;
- language prosody;
- transmission;
- amplification/recording effects in later periods.

## 11. Form

Possible structures:
- cyclic;
- strophic;
- through-composed;
- responsorial;
- additive;
- modular;
- process-based;
- improvised frameworks;
- cue-based;
- dance-linked sequences.

Use local concepts when documented.

## 12. Texture

Analyze:
- monophony;
- heterophony;
- homophony;
- polyphony;
- interlocking;
- layered ostinati;
- drone textures;
- solo/chorus relations.

Western labels are analytical tools, not assumed native categories.

## 13. Timbre

Timbre receives first-class status.

Methods may include:
- verbal descriptors;
- spectral centroid;
- attack/transient;
- decay;
- inharmonicity;
- noise components;
- resonances;
- comparative listening.

Quantitative measures supplement, not replace, culturally situated description.

## 14. Space

Music may be inseparable from:
- room;
- cave;
- courtyard;
- temple;
- open landscape;
- street;
- studio;
- club;
- radio;
- headphones.

Analyze:
- performer placement;
- listener placement;
- reverberation;
- distance;
- amplification;
- architecture;
- movement.

## 15. Movement and dance

When dance is integral:
- timing relationship;
- gesture;
- step cycle;
- body percussion;
- cue relationships;
- spatial pattern.

Avoid treating dance as visual accompaniment to “the real music”.

## 16. Transmission

Document:
- oral/aural learning;
- imitation;
- apprenticeship;
- notation;
- mnemonic syllables;
- institutional training;
- recordings;
- digital tutorials;
- software/templates.

## 17. Transcription policy

No single notation system is universal.

Choose the representation that answers the research question.

Possible:
- staff notation;
- percussion grids;
- cyclical diagrams;
- tablature;
- local notation;
- spectrogram;
- piano-roll;
- onset timeline;
- prose/phonetic description.

Always state what is lost in transcription.

## 18. HAZE analytical audio

HAZE may publish:
- full example;
- isolated parts where rights allow;
- slowed example;
- annotated timeline;
- click alignment;
- spectrum;
- comparative timbres;
- alternative analytical hearing;
- reconstruction.

Transformations are labeled.

## 19. Historical recordings

For recorded media:
- carrier;
- recording date;
- recording location;
- engineer/collector;
- performers;
- matrix/catalogue number;
- label/archive;
- transfer source;
- speed/pitch uncertainty;
- restoration steps;
- colonial/archival context where relevant.

## 20. Scholarly disagreement

Music analysis is not always singular.

Hazewave should publish:
- interpretation A;
- interpretation B;
- evidence for each;
- why terminology differs;
- what listening changes under each model.

## 21. Prehistoric music boundary

For deep prehistory:
- body percussion may be plausible but materially invisible;
- tool sound is not automatically music;
- resonant stone is not automatically an instrument;
- wear does not automatically prove musical intent;
- possible acoustics do not prove ceremonial use.

Use the inference ladder.

## 22. Quality gate

A music-centered chapter fails if:
- historical context is deep but music analysis is superficial;
- local terms are ignored;
- a generic regional label replaces specificity;
- recordings are untraceable;
- notation is presented as neutral/universal;
- analytical controversy is hidden;
- the HAZE audio contradicts the written evidence status.
