# HAZEWAVE ACADEMIC LIBRARY
# Historical Narrative Protocol v1
## Evidence-bound immersion for university-level books

## Purpose

Hazewave Academic Library must do two things at the same time:

1. sustain university-level historical argument;
2. make the reader feel that the past is being lived from inside.

The narrative is therefore neither a conventional textbook nor unrestricted historical fiction.

The canonical form is an **evidence-bound immersive reconstruction**.

Working internal name:

# BITEMPORAL LENS

The reader occupies an historically constrained human viewpoint while the text retains a clearly separated present/future analytical intelligence.

The past is experienced.
The evidence is audited.
Neither is allowed to impersonate the other.

## Core narrative premise

> We live the scene as one of the people of the period, but we ask questions with the critical tools of the present and future.

This does **not** mean that prehistoric people are secretly given modern thoughts.

The modern/future consciousness belongs to the narrative lens.

Past people remain bounded by their documented or cautiously inferred historical context.

## Two clocks

Every chapter operates with two clocks.

### CLOCK A — INSIDE THE TIME

Present-tense experience.

Allowed material:

- what can be sensed;
- what the environment affords;
- what objects/materials are available;
- plausible embodied actions;
- plausible social interactions;
- spatial relationships;
- sounds that can reasonably exist in that environment.

Forbidden material:

- unexplained modern concepts;
- retrospective labels in characters' mouths;
- exact emotions presented as archaeological fact;
- invented beliefs presented as known;
- technologies not evidenced for the selected scene coordinate;
- modern moral/political categories projected as if native to the period.

### CLOCK B — THE ANALYTICAL PRESENT

The narrative can stop, zoom out and ask:

- what evidence supports this scene?
- what is inference?
- what alternatives exist?
- what do acoustics/archaeology/cognition tell us?
- what terminology are we imposing?
- what would be anachronistic?
- what remains unknowable?
- how could future research change the interpretation?

The analytical clock is where the university-level argument lives.

## Historical empathy, not historical ventriloquism

The aim is contextualized perspective-taking, not pretending that we can recover private prehistoric minds.

A scene may reconstruct constraints, affordances and plausible decisions.

It may not claim:

> this person definitely believed X

unless evidence genuinely supports such a statement.

Prefer:

- “this environment makes X possible”;
- “this evidence is consistent with X”;
- “one plausible reconstruction is X”;
- “we cannot know whether X was understood this way.”

## Scene coordinate

No immersive scene is drafted before its coordinate is locked.

Every scene receives:

```text
SCENE_ID
TIME_RANGE
REGION
SITE / LANDSCAPE
HOMININ / HUMAN POPULATION (if supportable)
CLIMATE / ENVIRONMENT
KNOWN MATERIAL CULTURE
KNOWN FAUNA / FLORA
KNOWN FIRE STATUS
KNOWN SUBSISTENCE EVIDENCE
KNOWN SPATIAL ORGANIZATION
KNOWN SOUND-RELEVANT EVIDENCE
EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE
OPEN QUESTIONS
FORBIDDEN ASSUMPTIONS
```

If a field is uncertain, uncertainty is part of the scene contract.

## Evidence envelope

Every scene has an accompanying evidence envelope.

Each meaningful narrative element is classified:

- **DIRECT** — directly supported by archaeological/documentary evidence;
- **STRONG_INFERENCE** — multiple lines of evidence make it reasonable;
- **PLAUSIBLE_RECONSTRUCTION** — possible and context-consistent, but not demonstrated;
- **ANALYTICAL_THOUGHT_EXPERIMENT** — deliberately modern question applied to a constrained past setting;
- **FICTIONAL_CONNECTIVE_TISSUE** — narrative glue with no factual claim;
- **FORBIDDEN** — would overstate the evidence or create anachronism.

The published book may simplify labels for readers, but the production dossier retains the full envelope.

## Character model

Characters are **fictional composites bounded by evidence** unless a later historical period provides named individuals and documentary records.

A composite character may have:

- age range;
- kin/social relationships;
- bodily condition;
- task in the scene;
- learned skills compatible with the culture/material context;
- immediate goals;
- observable reactions.

A composite character must not be given:

- invented theology presented as fact;
- invented political institutions presented as fact;
- invented vocabulary presented as authentic language;
- modern scientific understanding;
- psychological diagnoses;
- unsupported social hierarchy.

## The “we” narrator

The canonical first-person plural is deliberately unusual.

“We” means:

- we inhabit the scene;
- we feel distance, darkness, cold, texture, danger and sound;
- we participate in work and social life;
- we notice musical questions because this series is obsessed with sound.

“We” does **not** mean:

- the historical community shares our future knowledge;
- we can communicate modern concepts to them;
- we alter history with future technology.

The narrator is best understood as a **bitemporal analytical lens**, not a science-fiction traveler requiring plot mechanics.

## Chapter rhythm

A strong chapter follows this rhythm.

### 1. COLD OPEN — INSIDE THE TIME

2–6 pages.

No lecture.
No dates dumped into the first paragraph.
Sound and material action establish the problem.

### 2. THE QUESTION

The scene generates one precise scholarly question.

Examples:

- How much can we learn in darkness through sound?
- When does repeated impact become intentional pattern?
- How do we distinguish tool noise from sound-making?
- What can a hearth organize socially?
- What can cave acoustics tell us, and what can they not tell us?

### 3. FREEZE THE SCENE

Shift to analytical present.

Define:

- date/region;
- evidence;
- disciplinary problem;
- competing interpretations;
- limits.

### 4. EVIDENCE DEEP DIVE

Primary/peer-reviewed sources.
Artifacts.
Spatial data.
Acoustic measurements/models.
Comparative evidence with caveats.

### 5. RETURN TO THE SCENE

The reader re-enters with more knowledge.

The same sound now means more.

### 6. HAZE LISTENING LAB

A companion experiment or audio asset.

### 7. WAVE FIGURE / MAP / DIAGRAM

A visual that resolves a real scholarly problem.

### 8. COUNTERINTERPRETATION

Present at least one plausible competing explanation when the literature supports one.

### 9. WHAT WE CANNOT KNOW

Explicit epistemic boundary.

### 10. FORWARD THREAD

One sonic question survives into the next chapter.

## Plot architecture

The book still needs dramatic structure.

The drama is not fabricated warfare or romance added to make archaeology entertaining.

The primary dramatic engine is **discovery of consequence in sound**.

Working macro-arc:

```text
SOUND IS ENVIRONMENT
↓
SOUND CARRIES INFORMATION
↓
THE BODY CAN CREATE SOUND
↓
VOICE CAN ALTER OTHER PEOPLE
↓
SPACE CAN TRANSFORM SOUND
↓
OBJECTS CAN BE CHOSEN FOR SOUND
↓
REPETITION CREATES EXPECTATION
↓
GROUPS CAN SHARE TIME
↓
SOUND CAN CARRY MEMORY / IDENTITY
↓
THE THRESHOLD OF MUSIC
```

Each chapter must permanently add one new capability or question.

## No “caveman” stereotype

Avoid the generic cave-person montage.

Early humans did not live in one universal cave culture.

Scenes must distinguish:

- open-air sites;
- rock shelters;
- caves;
- seasonal landscapes;
- different climates;
- different regions;
- different time ranges;
- different technologies.

Caves are used when the evidence/site requires them, not because “prehistory = cave”.

## Material reality gate

Before prose approval, WAVE research must answer:

- What are people wearing, if anything can be responsibly inferred?
- What can be held, carried, cut, burned, struck or eaten?
- What light exists?
- What does the ground feel like?
- What distances matter?
- Which animals are present?
- What season is plausible?
- Is fire present at this coordinate?
- What does the site preserve versus erase?
- What noises would the activities themselves generate?

If the scene cannot answer these, it is not ready for literary polish.

## Sound reality gate

HAZE research must define a plausible sound palette.

```text
GEOPHONY
wind / water / weather / terrain

BIOPHONY
animals / insects / birds where evidenced/plausible

ANTHROPHONY
breath / voice / steps / tools / fire / material impacts

SPACE
open field / forest / rock shelter / cave / water edge

DISTANCE
near / medium / far / occluded

TEMPORAL STATE
day / night / season / weather
```

The production dossier distinguishes environmental plausibility from direct evidence.

## Anachronism firewall

Every draft receives an anachronism review.

Check:

- concepts;
- metaphors;
- measurements;
- social categories;
- religion;
- ownership;
- family assumptions;
- gender assumptions;
- “art” and “music” as modern categories;
- technological vocabulary;
- geographic names;
- species labels;
- causal narratives.

Modern terms are allowed in Clock B.

Clock A should remain inside the material world.

## Presentism control

The reader is allowed to have modern ethical/emotional responses.

The narrative must not use those responses as evidence for what past people thought.

The protocol explicitly distinguishes:

```text
OUR JUDGMENT
≠
THEIR PERSPECTIVE
≠
ARCHAEOLOGICAL EVIDENCE
```

## Experimental archaeology rule

Experiments can demonstrate possibility and performance.

They do not automatically prove ancient intention or frequency.

Every experiment records:

- question;
- materials;
- reconstruction assumptions;
- controlled variables;
- realism tradeoffs;
- result;
- inferential limit.

## Archaeoacoustic rule

Acoustic measurements can characterize spaces.

They cannot by themselves prove why people used those spaces.

Required distinction:

```text
MEASURED ACOUSTIC PROPERTY
→ POSSIBLE AFFORDANCE
→ ARCHAEOLOGICAL ASSOCIATION
→ INTERPRETIVE HYPOTHESIS
```

Never collapse those four into one claim.

## Source hierarchy for scene building

Prefer:

1. excavation/site reports;
2. peer-reviewed archaeology;
3. dating / environmental studies;
4. specialist material-culture studies;
5. peer-reviewed acoustics;
6. experimental archaeology;
7. archaeomusicology / sound archaeology;
8. museum collections/catalogues;
9. carefully bounded ethnographic analogy;
10. secondary synthesis.

A cinematic idea does not outrank a source.

## Acceptance gate

A chapter cannot be called academically complete unless:

- immersive prose is compelling without fabricating evidence;
- the coordinate is explicit;
- central claims are sourced;
- uncertainty is visible;
- presentism is controlled;
- competing interpretations are fairly represented;
- experimental/ethnographic analogy is bounded;
- visual/audio assets have provenance;
- external subject review has been addressed.

## The signature Hazewave effect

The reader should repeatedly experience this sequence:

> I thought I understood this sound.

Then:

> the evidence makes it stranger.

Then:

> I return to the scene and hear it differently.

That is the narrative engine of the Hazewave Academic Library.
