# HAZEWAVE ACADEMIC LIBRARY
# Scholarly Source, Musicology & Verification Standard v1

## Purpose

Every Hazewave academic volume must be musically deep, historically rigorous, and independently verifiable by the reader.

The books must not merely cite “sources” in aggregate. Each consequential historical, archaeological, ethnomusicological, acoustical, organological, or music-theoretical claim must be traceable to the evidence used to support it.

## Core rule

> A reader must be able to follow the trail from a sentence in the book to the scholarly source, object, archive, recording, dataset, museum record, or experiment that supports it.

## Musical depth rule

Historical narrative is never allowed to flatten music into background color.

When a chapter reaches a sound practice, instrument, vocal technique, rhythmic system, tuning system, ensemble, recording technology, or production method, the book investigates the music itself.

Depending on the evidence, analysis may include:

- sound-producing mechanism;
- organological classification;
- materials and construction;
- playing technique;
- timbre;
- register;
- tuning / pitch organization;
- rhythmic organization;
- meter / non-metric organization;
- pulse;
- subdivision;
- polyrhythm / polymeter where analytically justified;
- groove;
- form;
- call-and-response;
- ensemble roles;
- improvisation;
- memorization / transmission;
- relationship to movement / dance;
- relationship to language;
- social function;
- ritual function where evidenced;
- performance space;
- performer/audience distinction or absence thereof;
- gender / age / status roles where evidenced;
- regional variation;
- historical change;
- contact / migration / exchange;
- recording history;
- transcription limitations;
- analytical disagreements.

## Terminology rule

Avoid using “tribal percussion” as a universal scholarly category.

In research and publication, identify the specific people, community, language group, region, historical period, instrument, repertoire, or performance practice whenever the evidence permits.

A broad phrase may appear as a reader-facing bridge, but the analytical layer must resolve it into specific traditions.

Examples:

- not simply “African drums”;
- identify Akan, Anlo-Ewe, Yoruba, Mandé, Kongo, etc. only where sources actually support the case being discussed;
- not simply “Indigenous percussion”;
- identify the relevant people/community and historical context;
- never imply that contemporary living traditions are unchanged fossils of prehistory.

## Organology

Hazewave may use Hornbostel–Sachs as one tool for instrument classification, while documenting its historical context and limitations.

At minimum, instrument records distinguish:

- idiophone;
- membranophone;
- chordophone;
- aerophone;
- electrophone where appropriate;

plus the local/emic name and classification when available.

The external classification never replaces the culture’s own terminology.

## Prehistoric percussion caution

Percussion-like behavior can be extremely difficult to prove archaeologically because:

- body percussion leaves no direct artifact;
- wood, skin and plant materials often decay;
- a stone/tool capable of producing sound may have had another primary function;
- repeated marks or wear do not automatically prove musical use;
- analogy with later or living cultures does not establish prehistoric identity.

Therefore, Hazewave separates:

```text
OBJECT CAN PRODUCE SOUND
≠
OBJECT WAS USED FOR SOUND
≠
OBJECT WAS USED MUSICALLY
≠
WE KNOW HOW IT SOUNDED IN PERFORMANCE
```

## Source hierarchy

For central claims, prefer the strongest available evidence.

1. Primary archaeological excavation/site reports.
2. Peer-reviewed journal articles and scholarly monographs.
3. Dating, materials, acoustics, bioarchaeology and environmental studies.
4. Archival primary sources for historical periods.
5. Museum object records and catalogues with provenance.
6. Critical editions / historical documents.
7. Ethnomusicological field research.
8. Organological studies.
9. Historical recordings / audio archives.
10. University / national-library research resources.
11. High-quality scholarly syntheses.

Popular media and unsourced websites may help discovery but do not carry a central academic claim.

## Reader-verification layer

Published editions should provide:

- numbered citations or footnotes/endnotes;
- full bibliography;
- DOI when available;
- stable museum/archive object IDs;
- stable public URLs when appropriate;
- page/figure references;
- discography / filmography / audio archive references;
- QR or short Hazewave URL to a chapter source page.

The chapter source page must never silently replace or rewrite an original source.

It acts as an index pointing the reader outward.

## Analysis of rhythm and percussion

When evidence is sufficient, the book can go as deep as:

- stroke vocabulary;
- interlocking parts;
- bell/timeline patterns;
- cyclic organization;
- phrase length;
- subdivision;
- cross-rhythm;
- polymetric interpretations and scholarly disagreements;
- lead/support relationships;
- dance coupling;
- tempo;
- timbral differentiation;
- drum-language relationships where documented;
- ensemble hierarchy;
- improvisation rules;
- notation/transcription choices.

No analytical grid is assumed universal.

Western staff notation is one representation tool, not the ontology of the music.

## Multiple analytical representations

Where useful, WAVE may publish:

- staff notation;
- timeline grids;
- onset plots;
- spectrograms;
- waveform/envelope views;
- cyclical diagrams;
- motion/dance alignment;
- instrument-position maps;
- local mnemonic syllables where authorized/documented;
- interactive audio-isolated parts.

Each representation states what it reveals and what it distorts.

## Living tradition rule

When the book reaches a living musical culture:

- use contemporary scholarship;
- seek sources from scholars/practitioners of that culture;
- distinguish historical evidence from present practice;
- do not claim direct continuity without evidence;
- preserve local terminology;
- document colonial collection histories where relevant;
- obtain permission for restricted/sacred material;
- do not publish recordings merely because they are technically accessible.

## Reader challenge principle

The book should make it easy for a skeptical student to ask:

> Where did Hazewave get this?

and answer that question within minutes.

That is a feature, not a threat.

## Minimum academic publication gate

A chapter fails publication if:

- a major musical claim has no traceable source;
- a culturally specific tradition is generalized into a vague “tribal” category;
- an archaeological possibility is presented as demonstrated musical practice;
- an audio reconstruction is unlabeled;
- a citation does not identify the exact relevant page/object/track when feasible;
- contradictory scholarship is materially omitted;
- the reader cannot locate the underlying evidence;
- the musical analysis is shallower than the historical prose for a chapter whose subject is music.

## Initial reference anchors

The research system should build outward from specialist literature rather than relying on these alone.

- Archaeomusicology / archaeology of music: interdisciplinary work joining archaeology, musicology, archaeoacoustics, organology and iconography.
- Hornbostel–Sachs: useful organological taxonomy, with historical and methodological limitations.
- Prehistoric sound-producing objects: evaluate object, context, wear, acoustics and alternative functions.
- Ethnomusicology: analyze named musical cultures in their own historical/social contexts, not as proxies for prehistoric people.
- Cross-cultural music research: useful for testing broad hypotheses, never a license to erase local specificity.

## Final principle

Hazewave is not writing a history in which the citation is decorative.

The citation is part of the architecture of the narrative.

The reader experiences the past, studies the music, sees the uncertainty, and receives the route back to the evidence.
